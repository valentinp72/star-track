Star-Track · alt-az mount · hardware

One bearing kit, used on both axes

Each axis gets the same parts: a 20 mm steel shaft through the extrusion, with a UCFL204GP flange bearing from Kramp bolted on each side. One hole is drilled per axis. Nothing is custom-machined.

Changes from the current build

The model follows the CAD model of the current build, checked against the real parts: a T of 30×60 bars (650 mm crossbar, 350 mm stem) on three levelling feet, and the same L. Differences:

  1. Both 95×70 cases come out, with their thrust bearings. Each axis gets a 20 mm shaft through the bar, with a flange bearing on each wide face.
  2. Azimuth. The shaft stands in a round-flange shaft holder (STHRB20) on the existing 95×70 plate, which still rests on the four 30×30 blocks. The motor moves from the base to under the far end of the bar, 152 mm from the axis, and the printed 250-tooth pulley lies on the holder's flange: four thumb screws through the pulley and a printed ring hold it, and the holder, down on the plate.
  3. Altitude. The shaft goes through the arm 70 mm below its top. The pulley and motor move to the outer face of the arm: the pulley is bolted to a second shaft holder on the outer end of the shaft, and the motor sits 152 mm below the shaft. The tube still hangs over the bar, held by its original clasp on an aluminium plate bolted to a third shaft holder. The two altitude holders are glued to the shaft with retaining compound, since their set screws alone barely hold torque.
  4. Electronics and battery. A smaller case without the touch screen sits lower on the outer face of the arm, below the altitude motor, because the outer bearing and pulley take the current case's place. A 12.8 V LiFePO4 battery sits at the far end of the bar. Both turn with the L. The joystick and buttons move to a hand unit on a cable.
  5. The L sits about 8 cm higher, because the azimuth bearing stack is taller than the current case. Near the zenith the back of the tube still clears the top of the azimuth shaft.
  6. Belts. Both drives use a 610 mm closed GT2 belt with a 20-tooth motor pulley and a 250-tooth pulley. That fixes the motor 152 mm from its axis. Each motor is bolted to an aluminium motor plate (Motedis, 3 mm), held off the L by a printed spacer; the spacer's T-nuts slide in the extrusion slot to tension the belt.

Why this removes the play

A thrust bearing only carries weight, so the axis can still rock on it. Two ordinary ball bearings about 75 mm apart on the same shaft hold the axis straight. Clamping them toward each other when the set screws are tightened removes the last bit of play. The thrust bearings and aluminium cases are no longer needed.

The bars stay as built: the 380 mm bar lies flat, and the arm stands on its end with its wide faces along the bar. Both shafts cross the 45 mm thickness, so each pair of bearings sits about 75 mm apart. That's plenty once they're clamped toward each other.

On azimuth, the shaft is fixed to the base and the L turns around it. On altitude, the shaft turns with the tube. Both motors are mounted on the L, and so are the electronics and the battery: no cable crosses the turning joint, so the head can turn round as many times as needed.

The bearings in the model now match the UCFL204GP's listed dimensions: 113 mm long, 60 mm wide, 33.3 mm tall, 11 mm flange, Ø12 mm bolt holes 90 mm apart. The holes would take M10, but 45-series slots usually take M8 T-nuts, so use M8 with large washers. Get the bearings and collar at Kramp. The shafts are plain bright steel rods (Ø20 h9, not hardened), so the holders' set screws can sit in a drilled seat. For the holders, search AliExpress for STHRB20 (round flange, 20 mm bore). Fitting it (the same on both axes)

  1. Drill a Ø24 mm hole through the 45 mm thickness of the bar, in the middle of the wide face, between its two slots. Use a step drill from both sides. It doesn't need to be precise, because the bearings hold the shaft, not the hole.
  2. Bolt a flange bearing on each wide face. Turn it 30° from the length of the bar so that one bolt lands in each slot: the T-nuts sit about 39 mm either side of the hole, one in each slot. Leave the bolts snug, push the shaft through, then tighten. The bearings line themselves up with the shaft.
  3. Tighten the set screws on one bearing. Push the other bearing's inner ring firmly toward the extrusion and tighten its set screws. The play is gone.
  4. Azimuth only: before fitting the L onto the shaft, clamp the shaft collar just below where the lower bearing will sit. It carries the weight.

Bar lengths, bearing heights and the base layout come from the CAD export (STEP). The telescope tube wasn't in it, so its size (900 mm long, Ø145 mm) comes from the earlier Fusion 360 design.

Taking it in the car

Split it into three pieces that come apart in about a minute, without tools. Turn on Pack for transport in the model to see them laid out, with their sizes.

  1. Telescope. Open the knob of its original clasp and lift the tube out. The clasp and its plate stay on the head. The tube travels in its bag or box.
  2. Mount head (the L with bearings, shafts, pulleys, motors, belts, electronics case, battery and clasp). Four thumb screws hold the azimuth pulley, and the shaft holder under it, to the base: undo them and lift the head off. The bearings stay assembled, so they keep their preload, and the belts keep their tension. A plastic crate with some foam protects the printed pulleys and belts.
  3. Base. The T stays in one piece with its feet and plate, and lies flat in the trunk.

Putting it back doesn't need to be precise: the two-star alignment at the start of each night absorbs any small shift. The battery travels on the head, so there is nothing to plug in between the pieces.
